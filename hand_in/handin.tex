\section{Building the Binomial Tree}
\label{sec:tree}

\begin{itemize}
  \item graph of small tree only prices (Mathis)
  \item explain how we build the tree (Juliette) DONE 
 \end{itemize}
We build the binomial tree as follows. The starting point is the closing 
price of Apple on April 28, 2026, denoted $S_0$. At each period, the 
price either goes up (multiplied by $u$) or down (multiplied by $d$), 
where $\Delta t = T/n = 0.02$ years. Both factors are calibrated using 
the volatility $\sigma$ estimated from Apple's daily log-returns over 
2020--2026. The larger $\sigma$, the larger $u$ and the smaller $d$.

Starting from $S_0$, each node splits into two branches: an up-branch 
($S \times u$) and a down-branch ($S \times d$). After 25 periods, the 
tree contains $2^{25} \approx 33$ million possible paths. At each node, 
we store the cumulative sum $C_t = \sum_{j=0}^{t} S_j$, needed to 
compute the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_n = C_n/(n+1)$.

At each period $t$, a recombining tree has $t+1$ distinct price nodes.
At $t=25$, this gives 26 distinct prices. However, since the Asian
option payoff depends on the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_n$, two paths
reaching the same final price can yield different payoffs. We therefore
track all $2^{25} \approx 33$ million paths separately, even though
they converge to only 26 distinct prices at maturity.

To compute the arithmetic average at each terminal node, we use the
cumulative sum $C_t$ stored at each node throughout the tree. At
maturity ($t = 25$), $C_{25}$ contains the sum of all prices from
$S_0$ to $S_{25}$. We then divide by $n+1 = 26$ to obtain the average
$\bar{S}_n = C_{25}/26$.

For each terminal node ($t=25$), we already have the final price
$S_{25}$ and the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_n$ computed in the
previous step. The option payoff is then $A_T = \max(S_{25} -
\bar{S}_n, 0)$. If the final price exceeds the average, the option
has value. Otherwise, the option expires worthless and the payoff
is zero.o

\section{Risk-Neutral Probabilities}
\label{sec:riskneutral}

\begin{itemize}
  \item intro why we need q (Erwan)
  \item proof of q (Erwan)
  \item explain relation with vol and interest rate (Erwan)

  \item Description of the differences between the two distributions (Juliette) DONE

Both $p$ and $q$ represent the probability of an up-move, but they
differ in nature. $p = 0.5$ is the physical probability assumed by
the model, meaning Apple has equal chances of going up or down.
$q \approx 0.491$ is the risk-neutral probability, derived from the
no-arbitrage condition. It does not depend on $p$, but on $r$
and $\sigma$.

The two distributions are very similar because $r = 1\%$ is small.
If $r$ were higher, $q$ would be further from $0.5$ and the two
distributions would differ more. The fact that $q < p$ means that
under the risk-neutral measure, the stock has slightly less chance
of going up than in the real world.

The arbitrage-free price of the Asian call option at $t=0$ is obtained
by applying backward induction over the entire binomial tree using the
risk-neutral probability $q$:

\[
  \boxed{V_0 \approx 14.05}
\]

\vspace{0.3cm}

This means that an investor should pay \$14.05 today to enter this
contract. Note that $V_0$ is lower than the mean terminal payoff
(15.71) because under the risk-neutral measure $q \approx 0.491$,
the stock has slightly less chance of going up than under $p = 0.5$,
leading to a lower expected payoff.

Our model relies on several simplifying assumptions that open the
door to possible extensions. First, increasing the number of periods
$n$ would yield a more accurate price. With $n=25$, the result is
an approximation and the larger $n$, the more $V_0$ converges to the
true value. Second, extending the maturity beyond 6 months would
allow us to analyse how the option price evolves over time. Finally,
using a stochastic volatility instead of a constant $\sigma$ would
better reflect Apple's real behaviour. However, this extension is
computationally demanding in our case, as the path-dependency of
the Asian option combined with a time-varying volatility would cause
the tree to grow exponentially.
