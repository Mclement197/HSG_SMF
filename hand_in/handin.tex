\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Asian options are path-dependent derivatives whose payoff depends on the average
price of the underlying asset over the life of the contract, rather than solely on
its terminal value. This averaging feature reduces sensitivity to short-term price
variation, making Asian options particularly prevalent in
commodity markets. This assignment focuses on pricing a European floating-strike Asian call option
written on Apple Inc. (AAPL), in which the strike is determined by the
realized arithmetic average of the underlying over the option's life.
We price this derivative using a multi-period binomial tree with $n = 25$ periods,
$T = 0.5$ or 6 months, and parameters calibrated on daily AAPL returns from 2020 to 2026
($S_0 = \$270.71$, $\sigma = 31.31\%$). Path-dependence is handled by augmenting
the state space with the cumulative sum $C_t = \sum_{j=0}^{t} S_j$, enabling
backward induction under the risk-neutral measure. Results are validated through a
normal approximation and a robustness analysis over the key model parameters.

\section{Building the Binomial Tree}
\label{sec:tree}

We build the binomial tree as follows. The starting point is the closing 
price of Apple on April 28, 2026, denoted $S_0 \approx \$270$. At each 
period, the price either goes up (multiplied by $u$) or down (multiplied 
by $d$), with equal probability 
$p = 0.5$. Both factors are calibrated using the volatility $\sigma$ 
estimated from Apple's daily log-returns over 2020--2026, using a 
historical method.

\subsection{Binomial Model Parametrization}

The size of the up and down moves is determined by the annualized volatility 
\(\sigma\) (annualized using $\sqrt{250}$) and by the length of one time step \(\Delta t = T/n\). 
Using the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein parametrization, the up and down factors are
\begin{equation}
  u = e^{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}} \approx 1.045,
  \qquad
  d = e^{-\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}} \approx 0.9567.
  \label{eq:up and down}
\end{equation}



This choice is consistent with the idea that stock prices are modeled through 
log-returns. Indeed, one up move corresponds to a log-return of 
\(\log(u)=\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\), while one down move corresponds to 
\(\log(d)=-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\). Therefore, the volatility of the one-period 
log-return scales with \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), as in a geometric Brownian motion.
Hence, the binomial tree can be interpreted as a discrete-time approximation of 
the continuous-time Black-Scholes model, where the stock price follows a geometric 
Brownian motion.

\subsection{Tree Structure and Path Tracking}

Starting at $S_0$, each node splits into two branches: an up-branch 
($S^u = S \times u$) and a down-branch ($S^d = S \times d$). After 25 periods, the 
tree contains 26 nodes and $2^{25} \approx 33$ million possible paths. 
The first three steps of the tree are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:tree}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{graphs/basic_binomial_trees/binomial_tree_3n_moves.pdf}
  \caption{Binomial tree with 3 periods. Each node contains the stock price at that point in time.}
  \label{fig:tree}
\end{figure}

In order to compute the prices at different point in,
we need to multiply the price at the privious node by $u$ (up move) or $d$ (down move).
The tree then extends upwards after an up move and downwards after a down move, as showin in Figure~\ref{fig:all_S_paths}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{graphs/paths/S_paths_25n.pdf}
  \caption{All paths of Spots ($S$) up to time terminal nodes ($t=25$)}
  \label{fig:all_S_paths}
\end{figure}

At each period $t$, a recombining tree has $t+1$ distinct price nodes. 
At $t=25$, this gives 26 distinct prices. However, since the Asian
option payoff depends on the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_T$, two paths
reaching the same final price can yield different payoffs. We therefore
track all $2^{25} \approx 33$ million paths separately by storing the
cumulative sum $C_t$ at each node.

\section{Arithmetic Average at Terminal Nodes}
\label{sec:average}

To compute the arithmetic average at each terminal node, we use the
cumulative sum $C_t$ stored at each node throughout the tree, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:tree_avg}. 
In order to compute the average at maturity, we apply the classical arithmetic average formula:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{S}_n = \frac{1}{n+1} \sum_{j=0}^{n} S_j.
  \label{eq:Arithmetic Average}
\end{equation}
At maturity ($t = 25$), $C_{25}$ contains the sum of all prices from
$S_0$ to $S_{25}$. We then divide by $n+1 = 26$ to obtain the average
$\bar{S}_n = C_{25}/26$. We use n + 1 in the denominator instead of n 
because we are including $S_0$ which means we have one more data point
than the number of periods.

  \centering

  \caption{Binomial tree with 3 periods. Each node contains the stock price and the average at that point in time.}
  \label{fig:tree_avg}
\end{figure}

This figure illustrates the first three periods of the binomial tree, where each node

nodes with $S=270.71$) carry different averages (283.01 vs 259.88), confirming that 
the arithmetic average is path-dependent and cannot be recovered from the current 
stock price alone.

\section{Payoff at Terminal Nodes}
\label{sec:payoff}

Asian options are path-dependent derivatives whose payoff depends on the average price
of the underlying asset over the life of the contract, rather than solely on its terminal
value. The payoff of a European floating-strike Asian call option can be written as follow:
\begin{equation}
A_T = \max\!\left(S_n - \frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n} S_t,\ 0\right)
\end{equation}
The option pays off when the terminal price exceeds the realized arithmetic average when \(S_T > \bar{S}_T\), a
structure that is path-dependent, as the payoff cannot be recovered from the
terminal price alone.


For each terminal node ($t=25$), we already have the final price 
$S_{25}$ and the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_T$ computed in the 
previous step. The option payoff is then $A_T = (S_{25} - 
\bar{S}_T)^+$. If the final price exceeds the average, the option 
has a strictly positive value $A_T > 0$. Otherwise, the option expires 
worthless and the payoff is zero.

\section{Risk-Neutral Probabilities}
\label{sec:riskneutral}

To price the call option, we cannot directly use the 
real world probability $p$, the true likelihood of an up or down move, because 
pricing under $\mathbb{P}$ would require knowing investors' risk preferences, which is not possible. 
Instead, we work with the \textit{risk-neutral probability} $q$, an artificial 
probability measure under which all assets earn the risk-free rate $r$ in 
expectation. Specifically, $q$ is constructed so that the discounted stock price 
is a martingale:
\[
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\frac{S_1}{1+r}\right] = S_0,
\]
which means the stock offers no expected excess return under $\mathbb{Q}$. 
This martingale property is central to our project: it allows us to price the 
option as a discounted expectation under $\mathbb{Q}$,
\[
    V_0 = \frac{1}{(1+r)}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1],
\]
without specifying any utility function or risk premium. In the remainder of 
this section, we first derive the formula for $q$ from first principles using 
the no-arbitrage replicating portfolio argument covered in lecture, examine how 
$q$ depends on the volatility parameters $u$, $d$ and the interest rate $r$, 
and then apply it to compute the risk-neutral probabilities for Apple's 
binomial tree.

\subsection{Proving the formula of Q}
-> AJOUTER SINGLE PERIOD MODEL + SELF FINANCING

\noindent To derive the risk-neutral probability $q$, we consider an investor who allocates 
wealth between the stock $S$ and the bond $B$ by choosing a portfolio 
$H = (\alpha, \beta)$. The portfolio value at $t=1$ is:

\vspace{1em}
\[
V_1 = \alpha S_1 + \beta \cdot B_1 =
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
\alpha \cdot S_0 \cdot u + \beta B_1 = X_{\text{up}} \\[4pt]
\alpha \cdot S_0 \cdot d + \beta B_1 = X_{\text{down}}
\end{array}
\right.
\]
\vspace{1em}

\noindent The investor maximises expected utility $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[u(V_1)]$ subject to the budget 
constraint $\alpha S_0 + \beta B_0 = V_0$. 
-> FIX ISSUE FORMATING

\vspace{2em}

\noindent \uline{The investor's optimisation problem is thus:}

\[
\max_{\alpha,\, \beta} \; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u\!\left(\alpha S_1 + \beta \cdot B_1\right)\right]
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad
\alpha S_0 + \beta B_0 = V_0
\]
\vspace{0.1em}

\noindent {\small where the constraint states that the cost of the portfolio today equals
initial wealth $V_0$. We maximise using the Lagrangian.}
\vspace{0.1em}

\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}(\alpha, \beta, \lambda)
  &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u\!\left(\alpha S_1 + \beta B_1\right)\right]
     - \lambda\!\left(\alpha S_0 + \beta B_0 - V_0\right) \\[6pt]
  &= p \cdot u(X_{\text{up}}) + (1-p) \cdot u(X_{\text{down}})
     - \lambda\!\left(\alpha S_0 + \beta B_0 - V_0\right) \\[6pt]
  &= p \cdot u(\alpha S_0 \cdot u + \beta B_1)
     + (1-p) \cdot u(\alpha S_0 \cdot d + \beta B_1)
     - \lambda\!\left(\alpha S_0 + \beta B_0 - V_0\right)
\end{align*}

\vspace{1em}

\noindent Taking first-order conditions with respect to $\alpha$ and $\beta$:
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}_\alpha
  &= p \cdot u'(X_{\text{up}}) \cdot S_0 \cdot u + (1-p) \cdot u'(X_{\text{down}}) \cdot S_0 \cdot d
     - \lambda \cdot S_0 = 0
  \\[4pt]
  &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u'(V_1) \cdot S_1\right] = \lambda S_0
\end{align*}

\noindent {\small Isolating $S_0$:}
\[
S_0 = \frac{1}{\lambda} \cdot \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u'(V_1) \cdot S_1\right]
\tag{I}
\]

\vspace{2em}

\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}_\beta
  &= p \cdot u'(X_{\text{up}}) \cdot B_1 + (1-p) \cdot u'(X_{\text{down}}) \cdot B_1
     - \lambda \cdot B_0 = 0
  \\[4pt]
  &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u'(V_1) \cdot B_1\right] = \lambda B_0
\end{align*} 

\noindent {\small Isolating $\lambda$:}
\[
\lambda = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u'(V_1) \cdot B_1\right]}{B_0}
\tag{II}
\]

\noindent We normalize the bond price to $B_0 = 1$ (one unit invested today) 
and $B_1 = (1+r)$ (grows at the risk-free rate):

\[
\frac{1}{\lambda}
  = \frac{1}{(1+r) \cdot \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u'(V_1)\right]}
\]
\vspace{0.1em}

\noindent {\small Substituting this expression for $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ into (I):}
\begin{align*}
S_0
  &= \frac{1}{1+r} \cdot \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u'(V_1) \cdot S_1\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[u'(V_1)\right]}
  \tag{III} \\[4pt]
\end{align*}

\noindent We define the variable $Z$, which represents the marginal utility of $V_1$ divided by the
expected value of the marginal utility of $V_1$:
\[
Z := \frac{u'(V_1)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[u'(V_1)\right]}
\]
\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent Since $V_1$ cant take two values in the binomial model, 
$Z$ takes two values accordingly:
\[
Z_{\text{up}} =
  \frac{u'(X_{\text{up}})}
       {p \cdot u'(X_{\text{up}}) + (1-p) \cdot u'(X_{\text{down}})}
\qquad\qquad
Z_{\text{down}} =
  \frac{u'(X_{\text{down}})}
       {p \cdot u'(X_{\text{up}}) + (1-p) \cdot u'(X_{\text{down}})}
\]

\vspace{1.5em}

\noindent $Z$ satisfies two properties:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Since utility is increasing, $u'(V_1) > 0$, so $Z > 0$ 
        in both states.

  \vspace{0.2em}

  \item The expected value of $Z$ is equal to 1 by construction:
        \[
          \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Z]
          = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\frac{u'(V_1)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[u'(V_1)\right]}\right]
          = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[u'(V_1)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[u'(V_1)\right]}
          = 1
        \]
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{1.5em}

\noindent {\small Using the two properties of $Z$, we can rewrite equation (III) as:}
\[
S_0 = \frac{1}{1+r} \cdot \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[Z \cdot S_1\right]
\]

\noindent {\small Expanding $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Z \cdot S_1]$ explicitly in the 
binomial model, where $S_1 = S_0 u$ in the up state and $S_1 = S_0 d$ 
in the down state:}
\begin{align*}
 & \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[Z \cdot S_1\right] 
  = p \cdot Z_{\text{up}} \cdot S_0 \cdot u + (1-p) \cdot Z_{\text{down}} \cdot S_0 \cdot d \\[0.5pt]
\\[-8pt]
& S_0 = \frac{1}{1+r} \cdot \left( p \cdot Z_{\text{up}} \cdot S_0 \cdot u + (1-p) \cdot Z_{\text{down}} \cdot S_0 \cdot d \right)
\end{align*}
\vspace{1em}

\noindent {\small By dividing both sides by $S_0$, we get:}
\[
1 + r = p \cdot Z_{\text{up}} \cdot u + (1-p) \cdot Z_{\text{down}} \cdot d
\tag{IV}
\]

\noindent We define $q$ and $(1-q)$ as the real-world probability reweighted by how much the investor values a dollar in that state, which makes the investor indifferent between the stock and the risk-free bond:
\[
q = p \cdot Z_{\text{up}}
  = p \cdot \frac{u'(X_{\text{up}})}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[u'(V_1)\right]}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
1-q = (1-p) \cdot Z_{\text{down}}
       = (1-p) \cdot \frac{u'(X_{\text{down}})}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[u'(V_1)\right]}
\]

\vspace{1em}

\noindent This is valid probability measure because both terms 
are strictly positive (since $Z > 0$, $p \in (0,1)$), and they sum to 1:
\[
q + (1-q) 
  = p \cdot Z_{\text{up}} + (1-p) \cdot Z_{\text{down}} 
  = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Z] = 1 \quad 
\]
\vspace{0.1em}

\noindent {\small Substituting into equation (IV) and solving for $q$:}
\begin{align*}
1 + r &= q \cdot u + (1-q) \cdot d \\[4pt]
1 + r &= q(u - d) + d
\end{align*}

\[
\boxed{q = \frac{(1+r) - d}{u - d}}
\]
\vspace{1em}

\noindent This is the risk-neutral probability: the unique value of $q$ consistent with no-arbitrage, independent of any investor's preferences. 
\vspace{1em}

\noindent Now, we apply the formula in coutinuous time to compute q:

\begin{equation}
  u = e^{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}} \approx 1.045,
  \qquad
  d = e^{-\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}} \approx 0.9567.
  \label{eq:up and down}
\end{equation}

Therefore:
\[
q=\frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d} = \frac{(e^{0.01 \Delta t}) - 0.9567}{1.045 - 0.9567} \approx 0.491
\]
\vspace{1em}


\subsection{Relation with volatility and interest rate}

\noindent With the CRR parameterization $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$, the
risk-neutral probability $q = \frac{1+r-d}{u-d}$ depends on both the risk-free rate $r$ and the
volatility $\sigma$. Taking partial derivatives:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial q}{\partial r} &= \frac{1}{u-d} > 0 \\[6pt]
\frac{\partial q}{\partial \sigma} &= \frac{\sqrt{\Delta t}\left[2 - (1+r)(u+d)\right]}{(u-d)^2} < 0
\end{align*}

\noindent The first derivative is strictly positive: a higher risk-free rate increases $q$, since the
bond grows faster and the risk-neutral measure must assign more weight to the up state to
enforce $\mathbb{E}^Q[S_1] = (1+r)S_0$.
\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent The second derivative is strictly negative. Since $ud = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} \cdot
e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} = 1$, the AM-GM inequality\footnote{The AM-GM inequality states
that for any $u, d > 0$: $\frac{u+d}{2} \geq \sqrt{ud}$, with equality iff $u = d$.} gives
$u + d \geq 2\sqrt{ud} = 2$. Combined with $1+r > 1$, we get $(1+r)(u+d) > 2$, so the
numerator $2 - (1+r)(u+d) < 0$. Higher volatility widens the spread $u - d$, so the
denominator grows faster than the numerator, and $q$ decreases. When moves are
more extreme, the up state needs less risk-neutral probability to still price assets correctly.
\vspace{3em}

\section{Backward Induction and Option Price}
\label{sec:backwardinduction}

To perform backward induction, we priced the option by first computing its terminal payoff (or the value of the option at maturity) for each terminal node,
\[
V_T=(S_T-\bar S_T)^+,
\qquad 
\bar S_T=\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}S_t .
\]
Then, we moved backwards through the binomial tree. At each node, the option value is the discounted risk-neutral expected value of the two next possible values:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:q}
V_t=e^{-r\Delta t}\times\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[V_{t+1}]=e^{-r\Delta t}\left(qV_{t+1}^{u}+(1-q)V_{t+1}^{d}\right).  
\end{equation}

Using $q \approx 0.491$ found above,
the final price \(V_0\) is obtained by recursively applying formula~\ref{eq:q}
from maturity back to the initial node. Essentially we are computing the expected value of the option at each node until we reach $t=0$.

\noindent Following this procedure, we find that the value of the option at $t=0$ is:

\[
  \boxed{V_0 \approx 14.05115}
\]

contract. Note that $V_0$ is lower than the mean terminal payoff
(15.71) because under the risk-neutral measure $q \approx 0.491$,

\subsection{Comparison with Monte Carlo}

As a validation of our backward-induction result, we compare $V_0^{Bi} = \$14.05$ 
against a Monte Carlo simulation of 500,000 paths with identical parameters ($S_0 = 
\$270.71$, $\sigma = 31.30\%$, $r = 1\%$, $T = 0.5$, $n = 25$). The Monte Carlo yields 
a price of $V_0^{MC} = \$13.87$ (95\% CI: [13.80, 13.93]), a difference of \$0.18, 
or approximately 1.3\%.

This small discrepancy is consistent with the discretisation error inherent to a 
25-period binomial tree. The binomial model approximates the continuous-time 
Black-Scholes dynamics by restricting stock price movements to two outcomes per period. 
With $n = 25$ steps, this approximation is coarse, and the tree price slightly 
overshoots the Monte Carlo estimate. As $n \to \infty$, the binomial price converges 
to the true continuous-time price, and the gap would close. The agreement between both 
methods therefore validates our implementation.

extreme of the parameter space.

\section{Normal Approximation}
\label{sec:normal}

To approximate the price of the floating-strike Asian call, we approximate
the distribution of

\[
D_n = S_n - \bar S_n
\]
by a normal distribution,

\[
D_n \approx N(\mu_D,\sigma_D^2).
\]
Since

\[
D_n = \sum_{t=0}^n a_t S_t,
\]
with

\[
a_t =
\begin{cases}
-\frac{1}{n+1}, & t=0,\dots,n-1,\\
\frac{n}{n+1}, & t=n,
\end{cases}
\]
we compute

\[
\mu_D = \sum_{t=0}^n a_t \mathbb E_Q[S_t] \approx 0.6790
\]
and

\[
\sigma_D^2 =
\sum_{s=0}^n\sum_{t=0}^n
a_s a_t \operatorname{Cov}_Q(S_s,S_t) \approx 1229.94.
\]
Using the normal approximation, the option price is

\[
V_0^{N}
=
e^{-rT}
\left[
\sigma_D \varphi\left(\frac{\mu_D}{\sigma_D}\right)
+
\mu_D \Phi\left(\frac{\mu_D}{\sigma_D}\right)
\right] \approx 14.2649.
\]

This price is then compared to the backward-induction price. The approximation is useful
because it is computationally faster, but it is not exact because \(D_n\) is not normally
distributed; it is a function of path-dependent stock prices.

\subsection{Model Limitations and Extensions}

Our model relies on several simplifying assumptions that open the
door to possible extensions. First, increasing the number of periods
$n$ would yield a more accurate price. With $n=25$, the result is
an approximation and the larger $n$, the more $V_0$ converges to the
true value. However, this comes at a computational cost: the number 
of paths grows as $2^n$, which becomes very large very fast.

The graph shows that the option value becomes more stable as the number 
of periods increases. For small values of $n$, the estimated price 
fluctuates significantly, while these variations gradually decrease with 
more steps. The model then converges toward a stable value, and beyond a 
certain point, increasing $n$ only has a very limited impact on the 
result.

Finally, using a 
time-varying volatility instead of a constant $\sigma$ would better 
reflect Apple's real behaviour, and would allow us to capture the 
volatility skew and smile observed in Black-Scholes implied volatilities. 
However, this extension is computationally demanding in our case, as the 
path-dependency of the Asian option combined with a time-varying 
volatility would cause the tree to grow exponentially, as the number 
of nodes would scale as $2^n$ instead of $n+1$.
