\section{Building the Binomial Tree}
\label{sec:tree}

\begin{itemize}
  \item graph of small tree only prices (Mathis) DONE
  \item explain how we build the tree (Juliette) DONE 
 \end{itemize}

We build the binomial tree as follows. The starting point is the closing 
price of Apple on April 28, 2026, denoted $S_0 \approx \$270$. At each 
period, the price either goes up (multiplied by $u$) or down (multiplied 
by $d$), where $\Delta t = T/n = 0.02$ years, with equal probability 
$p = 0.5$. Both factors are calibrated using the volatility $\sigma$ 
estimated from Apple's daily log-returns over 2020--2026, using a 
historical method.

Starting from $S_0$, each node splits into two branches: an up-branch 
($S \times u$) and a down-branch ($S \times d$). After 25 periods, the 
tree contains 26 nodes and $2^{25} \approx 33$ million possible paths. 
At each node, we store the cumulative sum $C_t = \sum_{j=0}^{t} S_j$, 
needed to compute the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_n = C_n/(n+1)$, as 
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tree}.

  \caption{Binomial tree with 3 periods. Each node contains the stock price at that point in time.}
  \label{fig:tree}
\end{figure}

At each period $t$, a recombining tree has $t+1$ distinct price nodes. 
At $t=25$, this gives 26 distinct prices. However, since the Asian 
option payoff depends on the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_n$, known 
as the floating strike, two paths reaching the same final price can 
yield different payoffs. We therefore track all $2^{25} \approx 33$ 
million paths separately, even though they converge to only 26 distinct 
prices at maturity. To differentiate between paths, we store the 
cumulative sum $C_t$ at each node, which allows us to compute a distinct 
average $\bar{S}_n$ for each path.

For each terminal node ($t=25$), we already have the final price 
$S_{25}$ and the arithmetic average $\bar{S}_n$ computed in the 
previous step. The option payoff is then $A_T = \max(S_{25} -

has a strictly positive value $A_T > 0$. Otherwise, the option expires 
worthless and the payoff is zero.

Figure~\ref{fig:distribution_q} displays the risk-neutral distribution 
of up moves over $n = 25$ steps, approximated by a normal distribution 
with mean $\mu_q = nq$ and variance $\sigma_q^2 = nq(1-q)$.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{graphs/distribution_q.pdf}
    \caption{Risk-Neutral Distribution of Up Moves ($n = 25$ steps, AAPL)}
    \label{fig:distribution_q}
\end{figure}
$q \approx 0.491$ is the risk-neutral probability derived from the 
no-arbitrage condition, depending on $r$ and $\sigma$ rather than 
historical returns. Unlike the physical probability $p$, which describes 
the actual likelihood of an up-move, $q$ is a mathematical construct 
used to price derivatives consistently with no-arbitrage. The fact that 
$q < p$ is therefore expected.

Comparing skewness and kurtosis between the two is not straightforward, 
as differences in these moments capture not only beliefs about tail risks 
but also their pricing. With a skewness of $0.007$ and excess kurtosis 
of $-0.080$, the distribution of $q$ is nearly symmetric and close to normal.

\section{Backward Induction and Option Price}
\label{sec:backwardinduction}

\begin{itemize}
  \item explain backward induction depends on q and how we get the result (Mathis)
  \item graph of the tree with values (Mathis) DONE
  \item Presentation of our result (Juliette) DONE
\end{itemize}


To perform backward induction, we priced the option by first computing its terminal payoff (or the value of the option at maturity) for each terminal node,
\[
V_T=(S_T-\bar S_T)^+,
\qquad 
\bar S_T=\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}S_t .
\]
Then, we moved backwards through the binomial tree. At each node, the option value is the discounted risk-neutral expected value of the two next possible values:
\[
V_t=e^{-r\Delta t}\left(qV_{t+1}^{u}+(1-q)V_{t+1}^{d}\right).
\]
The martingale measure $q$ found above
\[
q=\frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d} \approx 0.491
\]
is not the real probability of an up move. 
It is the risk-neutral probability chosen 
so that the expected stock return equals the risk-free return. Therefore, 
the final price \(V_0\) is obtained by recursively applying this formula 
from maturity back to the initial node. Essentially we are computing the expected value of the option at each node until we reach $t=0$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[height=0.6\textwidth]{graphs/backward_induction/backward_induction_tree_3n.pdf}
  \caption{Backward induction tree with 3 periods. Each node contains the stock price, the average, and the option value at that point in time.}
  \label{fig:backward_induction_tree}
\end{figure}

The arbitrage-free price of the Asian call option at $t=0$ is obtained
by applying backward induction over the entire binomial tree using the
risk-neutral probability $q$:

\[
  \boxed{V_0 \approx 14.05115}
\]

\vspace{0.3cm}

This means that an investor should pay \$14.05 today to enter this
contract. Note that $V_0$ is lower than the mean terminal payoff
(15.71) because under the risk-neutral measure $q \approx 0.491$,
the stock has slightly less chance of going up than under $p = 0.5$,
leading to a lower expected payoff.

\section{Normal Approximation}
\label{sec:normal}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Values distribution (Mathis) DONE
\end{itemize}

To approximate the price of the floating-strike Asian call, we approximate
the distribution of

\[
D_n = S_n - \bar S_n
\]
by a normal distribution,

\[
D_n \approx N(\mu_D,\sigma_D^2).
\]
Since

\[
D_n = \sum_{t=0}^n a_t S_t,
\]
with

\[
a_t =
\begin{cases}
-\frac{1}{n+1}, & t=0,\dots,n-1,\\
\frac{n}{n+1}, & t=n,
\end{cases}
\]
we compute

\[
\mu_D = \sum_{t=0}^n a_t \mathbb E_Q[S_t] \approx 0.6790
\]
and

\[
\sigma_D^2 =
\sum_{s=0}^n\sum_{t=0}^n
a_s a_t \operatorname{Cov}_Q(S_s,S_t) \approx 1229.94.
\]
Using the normal approximation, the option price is

\[
V_0^{N}
=
e^{-rT}
\left[
\sigma_D \varphi\left(\frac{\mu_D}{\sigma_D}\right)
+
\mu_D \Phi\left(\frac{\mu_D}{\sigma_D}\right)
\right] \approx 14.2649.
\]

This price is then compared to the backward-induction price. The approximation is useful
because it is computationally faster, but it is not exact because \(D_n\) is not normally
distributed; it is a function of path-dependent stock prices.

The first figure shows the risk-neutral terminal distribution of
\[

\]
together with the fitted normal approximation

\[

\]
The histogram is computed using risk-neutral path weights. The normal approximation captures the main mass of the distribution, although the empirical binomial distribution remains slightly skewed.
The second figure plots

\[
x^+ f_{D_n}(x),
\]
whose area corresponds to

\[
\mathbb E_Q[(D_n)^+].
\]
After discounting this expectation by \(e^{-rT}\), we obtain the normal approximation price \(V_0^N\).

This is giving us a different in price \(V^{N}_0 - V^{Bi}_0 \approx 0.21\), 
which means that the approximation with normal distribution is good. 
This means a relative difference \(\frac{V^{N}_0 - V^{Bi}_0}{V^{Bi}_0} \approx 0.015\) or 1.5\%.

Our model relies on several simplifying assumptions that open the
door to possible extensions. First, increasing the number of periods
$n$ would yield a more accurate price. With $n=25$, the result is
an approximation and the larger $n$, the more $V_0$ converges to the
true value. However, this comes at a computational cost: the number 
of paths grows as $2^n$, which becomes very large very fast. Second, 
extending the maturity beyond $T = 0.5$ years would allow us to study 
how the option price depends on the time horizon, and whether longer 
maturities lead to significantly different prices. Finally, using a 
time-varying volatility instead of a constant $\sigma$ would better 
reflect Apple's real behaviour, and would allow us to capture the 
volatility skew and smile observed in Black-Scholes implied volatilities. 
However, this extension is computationally demanding in our case, as the 
path-dependency of the Asian option combined with a time-varying 
volatility would cause the tree to grow exponentially, as the number 
of nodes would scale as $2^n$ instead of $n+1$.
